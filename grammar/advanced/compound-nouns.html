Compound Nouns — Part 1

Introduction

A compound noun is a noun made by joining two or more words together: tooth + brush = toothbrush. The result names one particular thing, and you understand it as a single idea, not as two separate words. English builds new compounds all the time, so you need to be able to make them yourself and get the small details right — the spelling, the plural and the 's.

After this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognise the main types of compound noun.
  • Form the plural and the possessive of any compound noun.
  • Make up your own compound nouns and be understood.

1. What a Compound Noun Is

Form: a noun made of more than one word — a main noun + one or more modifiers. The order of the words depends on the type:
noun + noun — a traffic jam, a job interview
adjective + noun — a blackboard, the small print
verb-ing + noun — a training session, a washing machine
noun + verb — a haircut, a sunrise
noun + preposition — a passer-by, a runner-up
noun + preposition + noun — a mother-in-law, a commander-in-chief

What it means: The main noun says what the thing is. The modifiers say which kind. A traffic jam is a kind of jam, not a kind of traffic. In most types the main noun comes last, but not in all of them: a mother-in-law is a kind of mother, so there the main noun comes first.

  1. We sat in a traffic jam for an hour on the way to the airport.
    (= a long line of cars that cannot move.)
  2. There's a training session for the new system on Thursday.
    (= a meeting where you are taught how to do something.)
  3. Always read the small print before you sign a contract.
    (= the conditions written in tiny letters at the end.)

Native speaker note: Compound nouns are how English names things descriptively. Rather than invent a completely new word, speakers put familiar words together so that the name itself describes the thing: a coffee maker makes coffee, a boarding pass lets you board. This is why new compounds appear constantly and are understood immediately.

2. Two-Word Compound Nouns

Form: the words are written separately, with a space between them. The main noun is normally second, with the modifier before it — a departure lounge.
Plural: add -s to the main noun, the second word — a boarding pass → two boarding passes.
Possessive: add 's to the main noun, the second word — the project manager → the project manager's email.

  1. The departure lounge is on the second floor.
    (= the area where you wait before you get on the plane.)
  2. We've had three performance reviews this year.
    (= meetings where your manager assesses your work.)
  3. I've still got the estate agent's number somewhere.
    (= the person who sells and rents out property.)

Important: The modifier stays singular however many things are involved: a risk assessment covers many risks. A few compounds are exceptions and keep a plural modifier — learn these as vocabulary: sports centre, customs officer, clothes shop, savings account, careers adviser.

3. Hyphenated Compound Nouns

Form: the words are joined with a hyphen. These compounds fall into two groups: those built round a main noun that comes first, followed by a preposition (a passer-by, a mother-in-law), and those made from a phrasal verb, which have no main noun at all (a check-in, a follow-up).
Plural: if there is a main noun, add -s to it — a passer-by → two passers-by, a sister-in-law → two sisters-in-law (NOT two sister-in-laws).
If the compound comes from a phrasal verb, add -s at the end — a check-in → two check-ins.
Possessive: add 's at the very end, always — my sister-in-law's flat (NOT my sister's-in-law flat).

  1. The check-in for long-haul flights opens three hours early.
    (= the desk where you register and hand over your luggage.)
  2. Two passers-by pulled the driver out of the car.
    (= people who happened to be walking past.)
  3. We're storing the boxes at my brother-in-law's until we move.
    (= the brother of your husband or wife.)

Native speaker note: Hyphens are slowly disappearing. E-mail became email, and co-worker is now often written coworker. If you are unsure, follow whatever your dictionary shows and stay consistent in one piece of writing.

4. One-Word Compound Nouns

Form: the words are joined into a single word, which then behaves like any other noun — a timetable, a landlord.
Plural: add -s to the end of the word — a database → two databases. If the last part of the word is irregular, use its irregular plural — a postman → two postmen.
Possessive: add 's to the end of the word — my landlord → my landlord's number.

  1. My landlord is putting the rent up again.
    (= the person who owns the flat you rent.)
  2. The timetables all change at the end of the month.
    (= the lists of when trains and buses run.)
  3. I need the bookkeeper's figures before Friday.
    (= the person who keeps the financial records.)

Summary: Compound Nouns — Part 1

Point Rule Example
What it is A noun made of more than one word: a main noun + one or more modifiers tooth + brush = toothbrush
Types noun + noun, adjective + noun, verb-ing + noun, noun + verb, noun + preposition (+ noun) traffic jam, small print, training session, sunrise, passer-by, mother-in-law
Word order The main noun is usually last, but first in preposition compounds a traffic jam = a kind of jam; a mother-in-law = a kind of mother
The modifier Always singular, apart from a few exceptions a risk assessment, but a sports centre
Two words Plural and 's both go on the main noun, the second word boarding passes, the estate agent's number
Hyphenated Plural on the main noun, or at the end if it comes from a phrasal verb; 's at the very end passers-by, check-ins, my sister-in-law's flat
One word Treat it as a single noun: plural and 's both go at the end timetables, my landlord's number
Spelling There is no rule — learn each compound as a word departure lounge, check-in, timetable

Common Errors

1: Putting the main noun first

❌ I'll meet you at the stop bus.


✓ I'll meet you at the bus stop.

In a noun + noun compound the main noun goes last. Many languages do the opposite, so check the order every time.

2: Making the modifier plural

❌ We have to complete a risks assessment first.


✓ We have to complete a risk assessment first.

The modifier never takes -s. To make the whole thing plural, add it to the main noun: risk assessments, customer databases, policy documents.

3: Putting the plural -s in the wrong place

❌ Two passer-bys called an ambulance.


✓ Two passers-by called an ambulance.

When the compound contains a preposition (by, in, up), the main noun comes first, so the -s goes there: passers-by, sisters-in-law, runners-up.

Useful Tips

📌 TIP 1: When you can't tell a compound noun from an adjective describing a noun, try adding very. A very green house works, so green is describing the house. A very greenhouse doesn't work, so greenhouse is one thing — a glass building for plants.

📌 TIP 2: Don't try to work out the spelling — there's no rule and even native speakers disagree. Look it up once, then write the compound in your notebook as a single vocabulary item, with its plural next to it. That way you learn check-in / check-ins the way you learnt child / children.
