Compound Nouns

Introduction

A compound noun is two nouns that name one thing: a bus stop, a toothbrush, a risk assessment. You already use dozens of these. What matters at this level is building them yourself — seeing how the two nouns relate to each other, keeping the grammar right, and hearing the difference between a GREENhouse and a green HOUSE.

After this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell a compound noun (a GREENhouse) from an adjective + noun (a green HOUSE), and say each one with the right stress.
  • Work out what the two nouns have to do with each other — what it's for, where it is, what it's made of.
  • Use longer compound nouns accurately: keep noun 1 singular, and put the plural and the 's in the right place.

1. What a Compound Noun Is

Form: noun + noun = one thing
bus + stop → a bus stop

What it means: Noun 2 says what the thing is. Noun 1 says what kind. A bus stop is a stop, not a bus. The main word is always last — many languages do the opposite, so check the order.

  1. I'll meet you at the bus stop outside the school.
    (= a stop for buses.)
  2. I need a new toothbrush.
    (= a brush for your teeth.)
  3. Put your cup on the coffee table.
    (= a table for coffee.)

Important: Compound nouns are written three ways — two words (bus stop), with a hyphen (mother-in-law), or as one word (toothbrush). There is no rule. Look each one up and learn it like any other word.

2. Compound Noun or Adjective + Noun?

Form:
compound noun = noun + noun → stress word 1 — a COFFee table
adjective + noun = adjective + noun → stress word 2 — a low TAble

What it means: A compound noun names one thing. An adjective + noun describes a thing. Two quick tests:

  • Can you add very?  a very low table ✓  → adjective.  a very coffee table ✗ → compound noun.
  • Can you move it after be?  the table is low ✓  → adjective.  the table is coffee ✗ → compound noun.
  1. We keep the plants in the GREENhouse. / They live in the green HOUSE on the corner.
    (= a glass building for plants / a house painted green.)
  2. Write it on the BLACKboard. / Pick up that black BOARD and pass it to me.
    (= the board you write on / any board that happens to be black.)
  3. She's my daughter's ENGlish teacher. / He's an English TEACHer, from Leeds.
    (= she teaches English / he is a teacher and he's English.)

Native speaker note: Native speakers do this without thinking and could not explain it, but they hear it instantly. Stress the wrong word and they picture the wrong thing.

3. How the Two Nouns Relate

Form: noun 1 + noun 2, where noun 1 answers a question about noun 2

What it means: Noun 1 is not random — it always answers one of a few questions. Knowing which one lets you build compounds instead of only recognising them.

Noun 1 tells you…QuestionExamples
PurposeWhat is it for?a safety inspection, a waiting list, a filing cabinet
PlaceWhere is it?an office building, a border control, a factory worker
MaterialWhat is it made of?a leather briefcase, a steel frame, a paper copy
TopicWhat is it about?a budget meeting, a policy document, a customer complaint
TimeWhen is it?a night shift, a summer deadline, a morning briefing
  1. Everyone has to attend the safety inspection on Monday.
    (= purpose: an inspection for safety.)
  2. There's a two-year waiting list for that flat.
    (= purpose: a list for waiting. An -ing word works the same way.)
  3. He's been on the night shift all week.
    (= time: the shift that runs at night.)

Native speaker note: These patterns are productive — you can invent new ones and people will understand. If you can say a budget meeting, you can say a budget review, a budget cut or a budget crisis. Learn the pattern, not just the word.

4. Noun 1 Stays Singular

Form: singular noun + noun
risk + assessment → a risk assessment  (NOT a risks assessment)

What it means: Noun 1 never takes -s, even when the meaning is plural. An assessment covering twenty risks is still a risk assessment.

  1. We have to complete a risk assessment before the trip.
    (= it covers many risks, but we say risk.)
  2. All the addresses are in the customer database.
    (= thousands of customers, but we say customer.)
  3. She's been asked to write the policy document.
    (= it covers several policies, but we say policy.)

Important: A few compounds keep a plural noun 1. Learn these as exceptions: sports centre, customs officer, clothes shop, savings account, careers adviser, arms race.

5. Making Compound Nouns Plural

Form: there are two patterns.

A. Most compounds — add -s to the end:
risk assessment → risk assessments  |  database → databases  |  check-in → check-ins

B. noun + preposition — add -s to the first noun:
sister-in-law → sisters-in-law  |  passer-by → passers-by  |  runner-up → runners-up

What it means: Use pattern B only when the compound contains a preposition like in, by, up, of. Everything else uses pattern A.

  1. We've got two risk assessments to finish before Friday.
    (= pattern A: -s at the end.)
  2. Both my sisters-in-law came to the wedding.
    (= pattern B: -s on sister, because of in.)
  3. The flight was late, so the check-ins were all delayed.
    (= pattern A, even with a hyphen — in here is part of the verb check in.)

6. Adding 's to a Compound Noun

Form: whole compound + 's
sister-in-law + 's → my sister-in-law's flat  (NOT my sister's-in-law flat)

What it means: The possessive 's always goes at the very end — never in the middle. This is true even for pattern B compounds, where the plural goes at the front.

  1. We're staying at my sister-in-law's flat until the builders finish.
    (= 's at the end, although the plural would be sisters-in-law.)
  2. The project manager's report is due tomorrow.
    (= the report belonging to the project manager.)
  3. I've borrowed my flatmate's car for the weekend.
    (= one word, so 's simply goes on the end.)

7. Long Noun Strings

Form: noun + noun + noun + main noun
train ticket price increase = an increase ← in prices ← of train tickets

What it means: The last noun is still the main word. To understand a long string, read it backwards: start at the end and work left.

  1. Passengers are angry about the train ticket price increase.
    (= an increase → in prices → of train tickets.)
  2. Send your complaint to the customer service manager.
    (= a manager → of customer service.)
  3. She's applied for a job as a sales department assistant.
    (= an assistant → in the sales department.)

Native speaker note: Four nouns is the limit. After that, even native readers have to stop and think, so good writers break the string up. In a long sentence, the increase in train ticket prices is much clearer than the train ticket price increase.

Summary: Compound Nouns

Point Rule Example
Word order The main word goes last a bus stop = a stop for buses
Compound or adjective? If you can add very, it's an adjective a very low table ✓ / a very coffee table ✗
Stress Compound → word 1; adjective + noun → word 2 a GREENhouse / a green HOUSE
Relationship Noun 1 = purpose, place, material, topic or time a waiting list, a night shift
Noun 1 Always singular a risk assessment, a policy document
Plural: most Add -s at the end risk assessments, databases, check-ins
Plural: noun + preposition Add -s to the first noun sisters-in-law, passers-by, runners-up
Possessive 's at the very end, always my sister-in-law's flat
Long strings Read them backwards a train ticket price increase

Common Errors

1: Putting the main word first

❌ I'll meet you at the stop bus.


✓ I'll meet you at the bus stop.

The main word goes last in English. Many languages do the opposite, so check the order every time.

2: Making the first noun plural

❌ We have to complete a risks assessment first.


✓ We have to complete a risk assessment first.

Noun 1 never takes -s, however many risks it covers. The same goes for a customer database and a policy document.

3: Putting the plural -s in the wrong place

❌ Two passer-bys called an ambulance.


✓ Two passers-by called an ambulance.

When the compound contains a preposition (by, in, up), the -s goes on the first noun: passers-by, sisters-in-law, runners-up.

Useful Tips

📌 TIP 1: Ask yourself one question: is this one thing, or a description? If the two words name one thing, it's a compound — say the first word louder (a GREENhouse). If the first word just describes the second, say the second word louder (a green HOUSE). Use the same question when you listen: a stressed first word means the speaker is naming one thing.

📌 TIP 2: Read long noun strings backwards. Start at the last word — that's what you're really talking about — and add one word at a time going left. Train ticket price increase → an increase → in prices → of train tickets. This makes headlines and work emails far easier to follow.

📌 TIP 3: Don't try to work out the spelling — there's no rule and even native speakers disagree. Look it up once, then learn the compound as a single vocabulary item. The one time spelling matters is when it changes the meaning: a greenhouse is not a green house.
