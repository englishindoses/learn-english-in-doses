Compound Nouns — Part 2

Introduction

In Part 1 we looked at what compound nouns are and how they are formed. However, at this level, compound nouns can become much more complex. English stacks three or four words into a single compound noun (a customer service training course) and can even turn phrasal verbs into compound nouns (a follow-up). This is very common in journalism, business writing and everyday speech.

After this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Work out the meaning of complex compound nouns.
  • Use correct pronunciation to differentiate between compound nouns and noun phrases.
  • Recognise and use compound nouns made from phrasal verbs.

1. Long Noun Strings

Form: three or more nouns in a row, working together as one noun. The last noun says what the thing is. Every noun before it tells you which kind.
How to unpack one: read it backwards, from the last word to the first, adding prepositions as you go.
a train ticket price increase → an increase in the price of train tickets
office fire safety rules → rules about fire safety in the office

How many nouns: two nouns before the main noun is comfortable, three is the practical limit. Longer strings do exist in headlines and technical writing, but they are hard work to read.

  1. Everyone got an email about the staff car park charges.
    (= charges for the car park that staff use.)
  2. I'm on a customer service training course all next week.
    (= a course that trains you in customer service.)
  3. They've brought in new office fire safety rules.
    (= new rules about fire safety in the office.)

Native speaker note: Native speakers use long strings to save space, which is why headlines, job titles, signs and email subject lines are full of them. In normal speech and everyday writing they keep them short and use of once the string gets long.
headline: the council road repair budget cuts
out loud: the cuts to the council's budget for road repairs

2. Stress: One Thing or Two Words?

Form: a compound noun is stressed at the front. An adjective + noun phrase is stressed on the noun. That is what tells a listener you mean one thing and not two words.

Compound noun — stress at the front Adjective + noun — stress on the noun
a GREENhouse — a glass building for plants a green HOUSE — a house painted green
the WHITE House — where the US president works a white HOUSE — any house that is white
an ENGLISH teacher — someone who teaches English an English TEACHer — a teacher who is English
a BLACKboard — the board you write on in class a black BOARD — a piece of wood that is black
  1. Everything in the greenhouse died while we were away.
    (GREENhouse — the glass building. A green HOUSE would be a house painted green.)
  2. My sister is an English teacher in Valencia.
    (ENGLISH teacher — she teaches English. An English TEACHer would be a teacher from England.)
  3. There's a bus stop right outside the flat.
    (BUS stop — one thing, one name, so the stress goes on the front.)

Native speaker note: It's important to remember that stress alone isn't always enough to tell the difference and native speakers rely on context as well as stress to tell the difference between a compound noun and a noun phrase with an adjective. Nobody confuses The White House has released a statement with She lives in the white house next to the shop. Use stress when you speak; use context when you listen.

3. Nouns Made from Phrasal Verbs

Form: many phrasal verbs have a matching noun, and it comes in three shapes:
one word — a breakthrough, a takeover, a setback, a handout, a printout, the turnout
hyphenated — a check-in, a follow-up, the run-up, a write-off
reversed, with the particle first — an outbreak, the outcome, an income, the upkeep, a downturn, the output

There are no rules about which phrasal verbs become nouns or what form they take — it happens naturally over time. Look and listen out for them, and learn them one by one.

Stress: always on the first part, which is what separates the noun from the verb. I printed it OUT → I've got a PRINTout. We checked IN at nine → CHECK-in opens at nine.

  1. Bring a printout of your ticket in case your phone dies.
    (= a paper copy. PRINTout, stress on the first part.)
  2. There's a follow-up meeting on Friday to see how far we've got.
    (= one compound noun, made from the phrasal verb noun follow-up plus meeting: a second meeting to check on what was decided in the first.)
  3. The turnout was much better than last year.
    (= the number of people who came.)

Native speaker note: These are everywhere in news and business English — a downturn, a takeover bid, the outcome of the talks, a cutback, the run-up to the election. Ten minutes on the news pages, writing down every one you find, will get you a long list.

Common Errors

1: Building the noun string in the wrong order

❌ We have to read the new safety office rules.


✓ We have to read the new office safety rules.

Check it by reading backwards. They are rules about safety in the office, so rules comes last, safety next, and office first. If reading backwards doesn't give you the meaning you wanted, the order is wrong.

2: Making the string too long

❌ Please read the attached staff car park charge review committee report.


✓ Please read the attached report from the committee reviewing staff car park charges.

The first one is grammatical, but nobody can read it. Once you're past three nouns before the main noun, stop stacking and use of, for or a relative clause to break it up.

3: Using the phrasal verb where the noun is needed

❌ Can you send me the print out?


✓ Can you send me the printout?

The verb is two words — I printed it out. The noun is one word, a printout, with the stress on the first part. Each of these nouns has its own fixed spelling, so check it the first time you use one.

Useful Tips

📌 TIP 1: When a long noun string stops you while you're reading, don't stare at it — say it backwards out loud, adding prepositions as you go, and stop as soon as it makes sense. Customer service training course — a course, of training, for customer service. Done. It takes about two seconds once you've done it a few times, and after a few weeks you stop needing to.

📌 TIP 2: Split stress into two separate habits, one for speaking and one for listening. When you speak, push the first word slightly harder — that one small effort is what stops a green house arriving in someone's head when you meant a greenhouse. When you listen, don't strain for it. Let the rest of the sentence tell you, and keep going.
