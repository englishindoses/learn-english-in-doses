# Advanced Grammar Page: Content Guidelines

These guidelines describe the content, structure, writing style, and standards for every advanced grammar explanation page on the English in Doses website. Follow them closely to ensure consistency across all pages.

**This document covers what to write.** For how to mark it up, see `grammar-page-html-guidelines.md` in this folder.

**Who the lesson is for.** The learner is an advanced student. They are not meeting these structures for the first time; they are learning to use them accurately and to choose between them. Write for someone who can already hold a conversation.

Where the live pages disagree with each other, this document wins. Where it is silent, follow the most recently built lesson.

---

## Page Title

- The page title (h1) should be the grammar topic name.
- It should be clear and specific. For example: "That-Clauses and If/Whether Clauses" or "Passive Reporting Verbs."
- If the grammar topic is commonly known by a simpler name, use that. Avoid overly academic titles that might confuse learners.

---

## Content Length: When to Split a Topic

- **A lesson is about 30 minutes of grammar** — from the title down to the summary table. Everything after the table (common errors, tips, final activity) is extra on top of that.
- Judge the page by how much content it carries, not by how many sections it has. The number of grammar point sections is flexible: small, quick points can run to several short sections, while points that each need real explanation will fill the lesson in three or four.
- **If the topic is content-heavy, split it across 2 or more lessons** rather than shipping one long page.
- When splitting, each page should be self-contained with its own introduction, learning objectives, grammar sections, common errors, tips, sentence ordering activity, summary table, and MCQ quiz.
- Give each page a clear title that shows it is part of a series (e.g. "Nominal Clauses," "Nominal Clauses — Part 2," "Nominal Clauses — Review!").
- A review page can be added after a multi-page topic to bring everything together.

## Section 1: Introduction

The introduction is the first thing the learner sees. It must do two things: explain what the grammar is and list the learning objectives.

### Introduction paragraph

**The introduction is a couple of short sentences.** There is no exact number, but it is short. It does two things and then stops:

1. Says what the topic is, in simple, plain language.
2. Says why it matters to the learner — where they will meet it (formal writing, academic texts, everyday conversation, news reports), what it lets them do, or what goes wrong without it.

- No history of English, no "in this lesson we will", no warm-up. If it has run past four or five lines, it is doing too much.
- Do not use the grammar structure itself in the introduction unless it is very simple. The learner has not learned it yet at this point.
- Keep sentences short and avoid complex vocabulary.

**Example:**
> Inverted conditionals are an advanced grammar structure used in formal English and literature. Instead of using "if" at the beginning of a conditional sentence, the subject and auxiliary verb are inverted (switched around). This creates a more formal and sometimes more dramatic way to express hypothetical situations.

### Learning objectives

- Listed under the heading: **"After this lesson, you will be able to:"** — this wording is fixed.
- Exactly **3 learning objectives**. No more, no fewer.
- Each objective ends with a full stop.

**Objectives are measurable and about the learner using the grammar to communicate.** We teach grammar to improve fluency and communication. Almost nobody studies grammar because they find the rules themselves interesting, and the learner is not being trained to describe English.

There is no fixed list of opening verbs. What matters is what the objective is *about*:

- ✓ **Using the grammar**: understanding what a form means, choosing between two forms, saying something with the right effect, avoiding a mistake that changes the meaning.
- ✗ **The rule as an object of study**: memorising a list of verbs, explaining when a structure is used, recognising it in a sentence, identifying the parts of it, naming it.

*Understand* is fine as an opening verb — understanding a form and being able to use it is the goal. The test is the object of the verb, not the verb.

**Example:**
> - Use that-clauses to express facts, opinions, and reported speech in subject and object positions.
> - Form if/whether clauses to express uncertainty and indirect yes/no questions in different positions.
> - Choose between the two forms when the difference changes what the listener understands.

## Section 2: Grammar Point Sections

This is the main teaching content of the page. There will be **one or more grammar point sections**, depending on how many distinct structures or uses the topic covers. Each grammar point section follows the same internal structure described below.

### How many grammar point sections?

- Use one section per distinct grammar structure or use.
- Each section should focus on one clear idea. Do not overload a single section with too many different rules.
- **There is no target number.** How many sections a lesson needs depends on the grammar, and the limit is the 30 minutes of teaching content described above, not a section count. Small points — one verb each, one adverb type each — can run to several short sections. Points that each need real explanation will fill a lesson in three or four.

### Internal structure of each grammar point section

Each grammar point section should contain the following elements in this order:

#### a) Section title (h2)

- A clear, descriptive name for the grammar point or sub-topic.
- Examples: "That-Clauses," "Wh-Clauses," "Modal + Have + Past Participle (V3)."

#### b) Form

- Labelled with **"Form:"** in bold.
- Show the grammatical formula/structure clearly.
- Use lowercase for the formula (no capital letters).
- Use colour highlighting only if it genuinely makes the structure easier to understand.
- When the past participle is mentioned, always include **(V3)** in brackets.
- When the -ing form of a verb is needed, write it as **verb-ing**.

**Example:**
> **Form:** modal + have + past participle (V3)
>
> **Form:** had been + verb-ing

#### c) When to use it

- Labelled with **"When to use it:"** in bold.
- A clear, simple explanation of the meaning and when this form is appropriate.
- **Form comes first, then When to use it.** Use **"What it means:"** in place of "When to use it:" only when the point is about meaning rather than choice of use. Do not invent other labels.
- If there are distinct sub-uses (e.g. subject clauses vs. object clauses), list them with brief labels using **"Key rules:"** as a sub-heading.

#### d) Examples

- **Exactly 3 examples each time examples are shown.** No more, no fewer. This means 3 examples per group of examples, not per grammar point section. If a grammar point section has sub-uses (e.g. "passive meaning" and "describing a state"), each sub-use gets its own group of exactly 3 examples.
- Present examples in a numbered list.
- Every example sentence must include a **short clarification in brackets** explaining its meaning. This helps the learner understand the context without needing to guess.
- The target grammar structure within the example should be visually highlighted (e.g. bold or a highlight span) so the learner can see it clearly.
- Example sentences should be **short and simple**. The grammar being taught should be the only difficult language. Do not use complex vocabulary or complicated sentence structures alongside the target grammar.
- Examples should draw from **three contexts: work, daily life, and travel**. There is no need to label which context each example comes from — simply vary them naturally.
- Examples must sound natural. They should be sentences a real person might say or write.

**Example:**
> 1. **What he said** surprised everyone. (his words were surprising)
> 2. **Where she works** is confidential. (her workplace is secret)
> 3. **How they solved the problem** impressed the manager. (their solution was impressive)

#### e) Notes

Every note opens with a bolded label. There are three, and no others:

- **"Native speaker note:"** — what native speakers actually do: how the grammar sounds in casual speech, how they shorten or simplify the form, common informal alternatives, subtle connotations, what they avoid, or how they express the same thing in real life (sometimes by not using the structure at all).
- **"Important:"** — a restriction, an exception, or a rule that stops a predictable error.
- **"Remember:"** — a reminder of something taught earlier.

Rules:

- Include a native speaker note with each grammar point **as long as there is something of value to say**. If there is nothing useful, leave it out — never write one for the sake of having one.
- Add **"Important:"** and **"Remember:"** notes only if genuinely necessary, and never to repeat something already said.
- Keep a note to 1–3 sentences.

**Example:**
> **Native speaker note:** In casual conversation, native speakers often contract "could have" to "could've" which sounds like "could of." Always write "could have" — never "could of."

## Section 3: Key Differences / Choosing Between Forms (Optional)

This section is optional. It can be included when the page teaches two or more structures that could be confused with each other **and** when a dedicated comparison section adds value without making the page too long, repetitive, or boring. If each grammar point section already explains its own comparisons clearly enough, this section is not needed. Use your judgement — if the page already feels content-heavy, leave it out.

### Content

- A clear explanation of how to choose between the forms.
- A bullet list summarising the key difference for each form (what it emphasises or when to use it).
- **Comparative examples** showing the same or similar situation expressed with different forms. This helps the learner see the difference in meaning or emphasis.
- Comparative examples should be grouped together (2–3 forms shown side by side for the same scenario), with a brief explanation after each one.

### Heading and format

- Headings in use include *Key Differences*, *Key Differences: Choosing the Right Verb*, *Choosing Between the Two Tenses*, and *X vs. Y*. Pick whichever names the comparison most plainly.
- **There is no fixed format.** Some are bulleted contrast lists, some are tables, some are paired examples with a `/` between the two versions. Write whatever makes the contrast clearest.
- When a topic contrasts several forms, this comparison usually works best as the last grammar point, pulling them together.

**Example:**
> - **Must have:** Strong logical conclusion about the past (high certainty).
> - **Could have:** Unrealised possibility or ability in the past.
> - **Should have:** Unfulfilled obligation or better alternative in the past.

## Section 4: First Practice Activity

The first activity comes after the teaching content and before the summary table. **Sentence reordering — a drag-and-drop activity where learners put jumbled words into the correct order — is the default**, and most pages use it.

### Choosing the activity type

The default is not a rule. Plenty of topics aren't about word order at all, and forcing a reordering activity onto one of those wastes the slot. Choose by what the grammar actually demands of the learner:

- **Producing the form themselves** — a typed gap fill, where nothing is given to choose from. The hardest, and often the most useful at this level.
- **Choosing between a small closed set** — a drop-down gap fill, when the whole lesson is about picking the right one of three or four options.
- **Word order** — sentence reordering, when the lesson is about where things go in a sentence.
- **Form against meaning** — matching, when the skill is knowing what each form signals.
- **Anything** — multiple choice.

The activity types available are whatever modules exist in `js/` and `css/`; those two folders are the only authoritative list, and the markup for each is in `grammar-page-html-guidelines.md`. There are more types on the site than the advanced lessons currently use, and one of them may suit your topic better than the familiar ones.

### Section heading

- Heading for the default reordering activity: **"Practice: Sentence Reordering"**. If you use a different activity type, name the section after what the learner actually does.
- A short introductory sentence telling the learner what to do. For example: "Drag the words into the correct order to make sentences with causative verbs."
- The instruction line can add a hint about what to watch for ("Remember: the main word goes last").

### Number of questions

- **Usually 5, and up to 8** where the topic needs it.
- Whatever number you choose, the score shown to the learner must be out of that number. The count of questions, the score denominator, and the answers registered in the page's script all have to be the same number. Getting this wrong has broken pages before.

### Question content

- Each question presents a set of jumbled words that the learner must arrange into a correct sentence.
- The sentences must use the grammar taught on the page.
- Like the MCQ questions, these should cover the different grammar points from the page — not just one sub-topic.
- Sentences should be short and use simple vocabulary. The grammar being practised is the only challenge.
- For reordering, use 5–8 words per sentence at the start, growing to 7–9 by the last question.
- Questions should progress from easier to harder.
- Activity sentences follow the same realism rules as the examples — see **Writing Style Guidelines**.

### Use fresh material

**Try not to reuse the example sentences from the grammar points.** The learner has just been given those answers, so testing them on the same sentences tests nothing — and they are advanced learners who should be able to handle the structure with vocabulary they haven't just been shown. Vary the words and the situations; repeating the same handful of sentences down the page also makes the lesson feel repetitive. The odd recycled phrase is not the end of the world, but it should be the exception.

This applies to both activities on the page.

### Feedback

- Each question should have feedback for correct and incorrect answers.

## Section 5: Summary Table

Every page must include a summary table. This gives the learner a quick visual reference they can return to when revising.

### Content

- The heading is always **"Summary: "** followed by the lesson name.
- The table should summarise the main grammar points covered on the page.
- **3 or 4 columns.** Use clear column headings, chosen to suit the topic — not every table needs the same ones. Common shapes are *Form / Meaning / When to use it / Example* and *Point / Rule / Example*; other useful columns include Feature, Focus, Common time markers, Best for expressing, Use and Formality.
- Keep cell content brief — short phrases, not full paragraphs.
- **Every row must correspond to something actually taught above.** Nothing appears in the table for the first time.
- **This table is the revision aid.** A learner who reads only this table should be able to do the final activity.
- If the page compares two structures, the table should have one column per structure.
- If the page covers multiple types of a structure (e.g. several passive forms), each type gets its own row.
- The summary table should have an **id of "summary"** so the MCQ section can link back to it for review.

## Section 6: Common Errors

This section highlights mistakes that ESL learners actually make with this grammar point.

### Content

- **Maximum 3 common errors.** Choose the 3 most frequent and important mistakes.
- Each error follows this format:
  1. **Error title** — a short, descriptive name (e.g. "Using 'of' instead of 'have'").
  2. **Incorrect sentence** — marked with ❌ (this is the site standard; a few older pages use ✗).
  3. **Correct sentence** — marked with ✓.
  4. **Explanation** — a brief explanation (1–2 sentences) of why the error occurs and how to avoid it.
- Errors should be **real mistakes that ESL students actually make, and mistakes that are common among them**. Do not invent errors that are merely possible.
- The incorrect sentences must be genuinely wrong. The correct sentences must be genuinely correct.
- **The two sentences must be minimal pairs**: the same sentence with one thing changed. Do not rewrite the whole sentence — the learner should be able to see the single difference at a glance.
- Keep error examples short and focused on one mistake per error.

**Example:**
> **1: Using "of" instead of "have"**
>
> ✗ She could of told me earlier.
>
> ✓ She could have told me earlier.
>
> This error occurs because "could've" (contraction of "could have") sounds like "could of" when spoken. Always use "have" in written English, not "of."

## Section 7: Tips

This section gives the learner practical advice on how to remember and practise the grammar.

### Content

- **Maximum 3 tips.** Each tip should be genuinely useful and actionable. Don't write one to fill a slot.
- **Tips are strategies, not rules.** The rules are already in the grammar points and the summary table; repeating them here wastes the section. A tip is what to do when you're speaking and have no time to think, how to practise, what to write in a notebook, or a memory aid for a rule already taught.
- Each tip is labelled: **📌 TIP 1:**, **📌 TIP 2:**, **📌 TIP 3:**.
- Tips can include: memory tricks, study strategies, practice suggestions, ways to notice the grammar in real life, or advice about when to use (or avoid) the grammar.
- Each tip should be 2–4 sentences long.
- The section heading is **"Useful Tips"** (38 of 41 pages use it; a few older pages say "How to Remember and Practice", "Memory Aids" or "Tips").

**Example:**
> 📌 **TIP 1:** Think of conditionals on a probability scale: Zero (100% — facts), First (likely future), Second (unlikely present/future), Third (impossible past). This helps you choose the right form based on how probable the situation is.

## Section 8: Final Activity (Test Your Knowledge)

Every page ends with a quiz so learners can test their understanding immediately. It is a multiple-choice quiz by default, and the rules below are written for that.

**The MCQ is the standard final activity.** Write one unless another type suits the grammar better — choose it the same way as the first activity, described in **Section 4**. Whatever the type, it takes the same position at the end of the page and keeps the same supporting elements (score display, Check Answers, Try Again, Review Grammar link). The HTML for alternatives is in `grammar-page-html-guidelines.md`, Section 11a.

Where the two activities differ in type, the pairing most pages use is **sentence reordering first, MCQ last**.

### Section heading and introduction

- Heading: **"Test Your Knowledge"** (36 of 40 pages; a few older pages say "Quick Practice").
- A short introductory sentence telling the learner what the quiz covers. For example: "Choose the correct option for each question to test your understanding of passive reporting verbs."

### Number of questions

- **Exactly 10 MCQ questions per page.** If the final activity is not an MCQ, use 8–10 questions.
- The number of questions, the score denominator shown to the learner, and the answers registered in the page's script must all be the same number.

### Answer options

- **3 answer options per question** (labelled A, B, C). Use 4 only if the grammar genuinely needs it, and then keep the same number for every question on that page.
- **Every distractor must be a mistake someone would actually make.** No filler options.
- **Every distractor must be genuinely wrong** — not acceptable under any reading of the sentence.

### Question difficulty progression

- Questions should progress from **easier to harder** across the 10 questions.
- Early questions (1–3) should test basic understanding of the form.
- Middle questions (4–7) should test usage in context.
- Later questions (8–10) should test more nuanced understanding, such as choosing between similar forms, identifying errors, or applying the grammar in more complex scenarios.

### Question content rules (from MCQ rules)

**MCQ questions should:**
- Test the learner's ability to **use** the grammar correctly.
- Test the learner's ability to **choose appropriate scenarios** to use the grammar.
- Only test grammar that has been **specifically covered** on the explanation page.
- **Cover all grammar points** mentioned on the explanation page (not just one sub-topic).

**MCQ questions should NOT:**
- Test the learner's ability to **recognise** the grammar (e.g. "Which sentence contains a passive?" — avoid this type unless it genuinely tests understanding).
- Be overly complicated.
- Use difficult vocabulary not suitable for the learner's level.
- Include grammar points or rules that have **not been covered** on the page.

### Question style

**Question formats are open.** Gap-fills are the most common, but do not limit yourself to formats already used on the site. Any format is fair as long as it tests the learner's ability to **use** the grammar, never their ability to recognise or repeat the rule itself. Never ask "What is a compound noun?", "Which rule applies here?" or "What is this structure called?"

- Questions use **gap-fill format** (sentence with a blank: "_____") as the primary format.
- Some questions may use a **scenario format** (e.g. "A newspaper needs to report unverified information. Which sentence is most appropriate?").
- Some questions may ask the learner to **identify an error** (e.g. "Which of these is grammatically INCORRECT?").
- Other formats that work: which sentence means X; which reply fits this situation; which of these would you say to a colleague.
- Put the distinguishing word in capitals when the question turns on it.
- Mix these formats across the 10 questions for variety, but gap-fill should be the most common format.

### Feedback

- Each question must have **feedback for both correct and incorrect answers** — one entry per question, with none missing.
- Correct feedback explains **why the answer is right** in one sentence, reinforcing the grammar rule. Some pages open with "Correct." and others go straight to the reason; either is fine, but be consistent within a page.
- Incorrect feedback gives a **helpful hint** pointing the learner toward the right answer **without giving it away**. Ask a question or point at the thing to check — "Your listener has not heard about this laptop before. Which article introduces something new?"
- The incorrect-feedback rule is the one most often broken when copying an old page. Check it.

### Post-quiz elements

- A **score display** showing the result out of 10 (e.g. "Your score: 7/10").
- A **"Check Answers"** button to submit.
- A **"Review Grammar"** link that takes the learner back to the summary table.
- A **"Try Again"** button to restart the quiz.

## Writing Style Guidelines

These rules apply to all written content across every section of the page.

### Language level

- Use **simple, clear English** that advanced ESL learners can understand.
- Avoid unnecessarily complex vocabulary. If a simpler word works, use it.
- The **only** new or difficult language on the page should be the grammar being taught.
- Example sentences must use simple grammar and vocabulary. Do not combine the target grammar with other difficult structures.
- **Address the learner as "you".** Never "the student" or "one".
- **Terminology is minimal and always explained on first use.** Countable, uncountable, gerund and infinitive are fine. Anything narrower gets a plain-English gloss.

### Sentence length

- Keep sentences short. Aim for 10–20 words per sentence as a general guide.
- Break long explanations into multiple short sentences rather than one complex sentence.
- Explain the rule, then stop. If a paragraph runs past four lines, cut it.

### Spelling and language variety

- Use **British English spelling** (e.g. "recognise," "emphasise," "colour," "behaviour," "practise" as a verb, "organise," "travelling," "learnt").
- Use **neutral, international language**. Avoid slang, idioms, or culturally specific references that some learners may not understand.
- **Contractions are fine and preferred** — this is spoken-register teaching English.

### Punctuation — hard rule, no exceptions

- **Every single sentence must end with the correct punctuation mark** (full stop, exclamation mark, or question mark).
- This applies everywhere, without exception: bullet points, numbered lists, table cells, brackets, parenthetical notes, example sentences, clarifications in brackets, error explanations, tips, learning objectives, and any other text on the page.
- If it is a sentence, it ends with a punctuation mark. There are no exceptions to this rule.

### Terminology conventions

- When mentioning the **past participle**, always include **(V3)** in brackets afterwards.
- When the **-ing form** of a verb is needed, write it as **verb-ing** (e.g. "had been + verb-ing").
- When showing grammatical form/structure, use **lowercase letters** (no capitals).

### Tone

- Supportive and encouraging, but not patronising.
- Direct and clear. Get to the point without unnecessary padding.
- Professional but approachable — like a knowledgeable teacher explaining something to a motivated student.

### Examples

- Always draw from **work, daily life, and travel** contexts. No need to label the context.
- Every example must include a **short clarification in brackets**.
- Examples must sound **natural** — like something a real person would say or write. A bus you'd catch, an email that didn't arrive, a flight that was late, a report due tomorrow. No *John gives Mary the book*, no invented characters, no set-up sentences that exist only to host the grammar.
- **Be a little imaginative.** Vary the situations, and avoid clichés and sentences nobody needs, like "the book is on the table".
- Every example must **show clearly the structure it represents**.
- Exactly **3 examples each time examples are shown**. If a grammar point has sub-uses, each sub-use gets exactly 3 examples.

## Content Amount Guide

To help gauge the appropriate length for a page:

| Section | Approximate length |
|---|---|
| Introduction paragraph | 2–4 sentences. |
| Learning objectives | Exactly 3 bullet points. |
| Each grammar point section | 1 paragraph of explanation + key rules (if needed) + exactly 3 examples per group (a section may have multiple groups if it covers sub-uses) + a native speaker note where there is something worth saying. |
| Key differences section (optional) | 1 paragraph + bullet list + 2–3 sets of comparative examples. |
| First practice activity | Usually 5 questions, up to 8. |
| Summary table | One table of 3–4 columns, with 4–8 rows depending on topic complexity. |
| Common errors | Maximum 3 errors (title + incorrect + correct + explanation each). |
| Tips | Maximum 3 tips (2–4 sentences each). |
| Final activity | Exactly 10 questions if MCQ (3 options each); 8–10 for any other type. |

These are guides for the size of each part, not a target for the page. The page as a whole is measured by the 30 minutes of teaching content described in **Content Length: When to Split a Topic** — how many grammar point sections that comes to is up to the grammar.

## Page Flow Summary

For quick reference, here is the complete section order for every advanced grammar page:

1. **Page title** (h1).
2. **Introduction** — what it is, why it matters, 3 learning objectives.
3. **Grammar point section(s)** — form, when to use it, 3 examples, native speaker note where useful. Repeat for each sub-topic.
4. **Key differences** — comparison between forms (optional — include only when it adds value without making the page too long or repetitive).
5. **First practice activity** — 5–8 questions, sentence reordering by default.
6. **Summary table** — quick-reference visual summary.
7. **Common errors** — up to 3 real mistakes with corrections and explanations.
8. **Tips** — up to 3 practical, actionable study tips.
9. **Final activity ("Test Your Knowledge")** — 10 MCQ questions testing all grammar points from the page, or 8–10 questions if another type is used.

**This order never varies**, and the two activities always sit in slots 5 and 9.

## Final Checklist

Before considering a page complete, verify the following:

- [ ] The lesson is about 30 minutes of grammar down to the summary table. If the topic is content-heavy, it has been split across multiple lessons.
- [ ] Introduction clearly explains the grammar in simple language.
- [ ] Introduction is a couple of short sentences — what the topic is, why it matters — with no warm-up or history.
- [ ] Exactly 3 learning objectives are listed, about using the grammar and never about the rule as an object of study.
- [ ] Every grammar point section includes: form, when to use it, and exactly 3 examples per group of examples.
- [ ] Every example has a short clarification in brackets.
- [ ] Examples are drawn from work, daily life, and travel contexts.
- [ ] Example sentences use simple grammar and vocabulary (only the target grammar is new).
- [ ] Native speaker notes say how people really use or avoid the structure, not the rule again.
- [ ] Native speaker notes are included only where genuinely useful, and no Important or Remember note repeats something already said.
- [ ] Key differences section is included only if it adds value without making the page too long or repetitive.
- [ ] The first activity suits what the grammar actually demands of the learner, and has 5 questions (up to 8 if needed) covering the grammar points from the page.
- [ ] Every score shown to the learner is out of the actual number of questions in that activity.
- [ ] Activity sentences use fresh vocabulary, not the example sentences from the lesson.
- [ ] The page sits in the right place in the sequence: its previous and next links follow the lesson order on the grammar index page, and its neighbours point back to it.
- [ ] Summary table is present, covers all forms taught on the page, and adds nothing that wasn't taught.
- [ ] A learner who reads only the summary table could do the final activity.
- [ ] No more than 3 common errors, each with incorrect sentence, correct sentence, and explanation.
- [ ] The errors are real mistakes ESL learners actually make, and the two sentences are minimal pairs.
- [ ] No more than 3 tips, they are strategies rather than rules, and they do not repeat lesson content.
- [ ] Final activity has exactly 10 questions if MCQ, or 8–10 if another type.
- [ ] MCQ questions have 3 answer options each (A, B, C), or 4 throughout if the grammar needs it.
- [ ] Every question tests using the grammar, never naming or reciting the rule.
- [ ] Every distractor is genuinely wrong, and wrong in a way a learner would fall for.
- [ ] Questions cover all grammar points from the page and include none that weren't covered.
- [ ] Questions progress from easier to harder.
- [ ] Correct and incorrect feedback covers every question, and the incorrect feedback hints without stating the answer.
- [ ] British English spelling is used throughout.
- [ ] The learner is addressed as "you" throughout.
- [ ] Any terminology narrower than the basics is glossed on first use.
- [ ] **Every single sentence on the page ends with the correct punctuation mark — no exceptions. This includes sentences in bullet points, lists, brackets, table cells, example clarifications, tips, error explanations, and everywhere else.**
- [ ] Past participle is always followed by (V3).
- [ ] The -ing verb form is written as verb-ing.
- [ ] Grammatical forms shown in lowercase.
